## A) Body Dimension Framework

The user is right that ecto/meso/endo is too coarse for styling decisions. Three-bucket somatotype was developed by W.H. Sheldon in the 1940s for *constitutional psychology* (a discredited theory linking body shape to personality), not garment fit, and it collapses too many dimensions. The styling-relevant decomposition that emerges from the source set is **eight independent dimensions**, each of which drives a specific category of styling decision:

### 1. Shoulder width (relative to waist/hips)
This is the primary silhouette driver and the dimension that defines RMRS's three-shape system. The triangle taxonomy used by Antonio Centeno:
- **Inverted triangle** — shoulders wider than waist/hips (muscular/athletic).
- **Rectangle** — shoulders ≈ waist ≈ hips (slim/skinny, or "I-shape").
- **Triangle** ("dad bod") — waist/hips wider than shoulders.
The "ideal" shape men aspire to is rectangular-with-shoulders — Gentleman's Gazette: *"the rectangular body type [is] proportional width where the shoulders are about as wide as the hips, which is considered a very masculine style that most men aspire to. If you have this body type, there's hardly anything you can't wear."* Westwood Hart frames the target ratio as **shoulder-to-head ≈ 3:1** — your shoulders should appear about three times the width of your head.

### 2. Shoulder type (square / sloped / forward)
Independent of width. RMRS: *"Men can have broad, square or sloped shoulders and suits should be designed for your shoulder type to ensure proper fit."* Sloped shoulders need shoulder-pad support; square shoulders need minimal/natural shoulder construction (heavy padding looks like football gear); forward (rounded forward, common in desk workers) needs jackets cut with forward-rotating sleeves.

### 3. Torso-to-leg ratio
The most under-discussed dimension and the one with the highest payoff for styling intervention. Two cohorts:
- **Long torso / short legs** — shorter inseam than torso length. Add visual leg length: high-rise trousers, no-or-slight break, monochromatic lower half (shoe color matches pants), shorter shirt/jacket length.
- **Short torso / long legs** — longer inseam than torso length. The opposite: lower-rise pants are tolerable, longer shirt hem and jacket length OK, contrasting belt/shoes can be used to create a horizontal break.
Gentleman's Gazette: *"if pants sit at the hips, legs appear shorter and the torso longer, whereas high-rise pants make the upper body appear shorter and legs longer."* This is the single highest-leverage proportion lever in menswear.

### 4. Limb length (arms, legs) relative to torso
Long arms create persistent off-the-rack sleeve-length problems (sleeves end too high; cuff doesn't show under jacket). Articles of Style notes that for long-armed men, tailors often deliberately show **only ¼" of shirt cuff** rather than the standard ½" — a deliberate compression to make the arms appear less long. Sleeve-length is mostly tailor-fixable (most jackets have 1–1.5" of fabric inside the cuff for lengthening) but ready-to-wear shopping is harder.

### 5. Build (slight / athletic / stocky / heavyset)
Distinct from shoulder width. "Stocky" is the combination of broader frame + shorter stature; it has a different rule set than "muscular" (taller + broader). Modest Man's specific advice for short-stocky: *"avoid skinny fit whenever possible. Clothes that are too tight will just emphasize your size... go for slim-but-not-skinny pants that have a subtle taper below the knee. Look for 'slim straight' or 'athletic' cuts."*

### 6. Body fat / midsection vs. chest/shoulder ratio
Independent of build. A heavyset man with a barrel chest and thick neck dresses very differently from a heavyset man with a soft midsection and narrow shoulders. The barrel-chested cohort needs jackets cut for chest, then everything else tailored down; the soft-middle cohort needs structured pieces that draw the eye up and away from the midsection (RMRS: *"draw the eye upward and present even proportions"*).

### 7. Head size relative to body + neck length
Westwood Hart and Styleforum threads converge: a larger head reads as out-of-proportion if shoulders are narrow. Compensations: wider lapels, wider tie knots (Half/Full Windsor instead of four-in-hand), structured jackets that extend past natural shoulder, larger collars. *"A bigger head rates wider lapels, and you may want to have the shoulders extended beyond your natural shoulder to minimize your head size."* A small head + broad shoulders is the opposite problem and is rarer; tighter collars, narrower lapels, smaller knots correct it. **Neck length** drives collar choice independently — short neck = lower collar band, more spread (cutaway/spread), open top button when possible; long neck = taller collar band, longer points, button up.

### 8. Skin undertone × hair color × eye color (the contrast dimension)
Treated separately in Section C. This is the *color* axis of the framework rather than the silhouette axis, but it's a true independent dimension — two men with identical bodies can require very different palettes.

### What this means for the assessment
A defensible Cleanmaxxing decision matrix needs to capture, at minimum: shoulder width (broad/proportional/narrow), shoulder type (square/sloped, optional), torso-to-leg (long-torso/balanced/short-torso), build (slight/athletic/stocky/heavyset), midsection (lean/soft/barrel), head-to-shoulder feel (proportional/large head/small head, optional), height bucket (under 5'8" / 5'8"-6'0" / over 6'0"), and the color trio (undertone, hair, contrast). That's seven to eight axes — tractable for a quiz, dramatically more powerful than three-letter somatotype.

## B) Silhouette Rules Per Body Dimension

These are declarative, citable rules. Each is grounded in at least one source from the list above.

### Broad shoulders (inverted triangle / muscular)
- **DO** wear V-necks and open-collar shirts to draw the eye downward and create a longer line — counterintuitive given the "fill out the V" instinct, but cited by Stitchfix as a balance technique because uninterrupted vertical visual lines look longer.
- **DO** wear straight-cut or moderately-tapered pants to add visible volume to the lower half. RMRS: *"avoid skinny jeans and extra slim fitted trousers because these will accentuate your legs and make your top half look disproportionately wide."*
- **DO** wear single-breasted, two-button jackets with moderate lapels.
- **DON'T** wear epaulettes, heavy shoulder padding, horizontal stripes across the chest, large chest logos, or chunky sweaters. All exaggerate width on a frame that's already wide.
- **DON'T** wear tight crew necks — they compress the traps and visually widen the chest line further.
- **The fit problem**: ready-to-wear shirts that fit shoulders are loose at the waist, and shirts that fit the waist pull at the chest/shoulders. Solution: buy for shoulders/chest, tailor the waist down. This is easy tailoring.

### Narrow shoulders (rectangle / slight build)
- **DO** add upper-body volume: layering (cardigan + shirt + sport jacket), shoulder-padded blazers, structured overcoats, two-piece yokes on shirts.
- **DO** use horizontal stripes across the chest area — the only body type for which they consistently flatter.
- **DO** keep pants slim/tapered to amplify shoulder-to-leg contrast.
- **DON'T** wear baggy/oversized tops — they fall off the shoulders and emphasize the slightness rather than concealing it.
- **DON'T** wear deep V-necks — they extend the visual line of the chest and read narrower still.
- RMRS: *"create the illusion of broader shoulders and a slimmer waist. Horizontal stripes, especially across your upper torso, will add width to your slight frame."*

### Soft midsection / "dad bod" (triangle, with weight at waist)
- **DO** wear single-breasted, V-front, two-button jackets — the deep V lengthens and narrows the visible torso.
- **DON'T** wear double-breasted jackets — the closed cross-front squares off the silhouette and adds visual bulk.
- **DON'T** wear polos, roll-necks, or fitted knits that cling at the waist while narrowing the shoulders.
- **DON'T** wear pleats or cuffs on pants — both visually weigh the legs down.
- Stick with classic-fit pants (no slim/skinny) and structured upper layers that draw the eye up.

### Stocky / barrel-chested (broad and short)
- Fit to your largest area (chest), then tailor everything else down. Modest Man: *"find a shirt that fits shoulders and neck, then getting the length and sleeves shortened is a breeze."*
- Watch for the **chest button gap** — the second-from-top button often sits at the widest point of a barrel chest and pulls open. Either size up and tailor, or have a tailor relocate the button.
- Wider tie knots (Half Windsor, Full Windsor) — a skinny tie with a small knot looks visually disconnected from a thick neck.
- Vertical-line everything: pinstripes, vertical-rib knits, tonal monochrome.
- Avoid skinny anything; avoid wide-spread collars on already-thick necks; avoid bulky horizontal patterns.

### Long torso / short legs
- High-rise trousers (sit at the natural waist, finger-width below belly button). Single highest-leverage move.
- Shirt hem ends just below belt line — never longer.
- Match shoe color to pant color (low contrast = continuous visual line = longer leg).
- Slim belt, not chunky, in matched color.
- Shorter jacket length (ends at the hip, not below).
- No break or slight break on pants.

### Short torso / long legs
- Lower-rise pants are tolerable.
- Longer shirt hem (to the mid-fly) and longer jacket length (covering seat fully) help re-balance.
- Contrasting belt or contrasting shoe color is a tool here, not a mistake — it creates the missing horizontal break.
- Half or full break on pants.

### Long arms
- The persistent shopping problem: standard-length sleeves come up short. Buy "long" sized shirts/jackets when available; otherwise a tailor can typically add 0.5–1" to a jacket sleeve from the inside-cuff allowance.
- Show less shirt cuff under the jacket (¼" instead of standard ½") to compress the visual length of the arm.

### Big head (head appears large for shoulders)
- Wider lapels, structured/extended shoulders, larger collar points, fatter tie knots.
- Avoid tight-fitting slim-cut tops that narrow the shoulders.
- Lighter colors on top can visually enlarge the upper body to balance the head.

### Short stature (under 5'8")
- High-rise trousers (the leg-lengthening lever again).
- Slim-fit but never skinny.
- Hip-length jackets, never below; single-breasted, not double.
- Monochrome or tonal outfits; vertical stripes; matched belt-and-shoe color.
- Smaller-scale accessories: 38–40mm watches max, slim belts, narrow ties.
- RMRS: *"if you have to choose between elongating the visual impression of your torso and your legs, legs take precedence."*

### Tall (over 6'2")
- Half/full break on pants instead of no break.
- Longer jacket length is allowed — the seat must be covered, but tall men can run slightly past.
- Horizontal breaks (contrasting belt, contrasting shoe color) are a styling option, not a mistake — they prevent the silhouette reading as elongated.

## C) Color Theory — Practical Version

### Determining undertone (proxy questions)

**Vein test** (most cited, not always conclusive):
- Bluish/purple veins on inner wrist → cool undertone.
- Greenish veins → warm undertone.
- Both / can't tell → neutral undertone.

**Jewelry test** (high signal):
- Silver jewelry flatters more than gold → cool.
- Gold jewelry flatters more than silver → warm.
- Both look fine → neutral.

**Sun reaction test** (most actionable for the assessment):
- Burn easily, struggle to tan → cool undertone.
- Tan easily, rarely burn → warm undertone.
- Burn first then tan → neutral.

**White-paper test** (good for in-person, hard for an app):
- Skin reads pink/rosy/bluish next to white → cool.
- Skin reads golden/peach/yellow → warm.
- Reads even → neutral.

For the Cleanmaxxing assessment, **the jewelry test + sun reaction test together** likely give the cleanest signal without requiring a mirror or specific lighting.

### Color recommendations by undertone

**Cool undertone** (pink/blue/red base in skin):
- Best: blues (any depth), purples, true white, cool reds (blue-based reds — burgundy works), emerald green, pink, gray, charcoal.
- Avoid: yellow, orange, warm browns, mustard, peach. They make cool-toned skin look washed out or jaundiced.
- Silver, white-gold, platinum jewelry. Cool gray suiting.

**Warm undertone** (yellow/golden/peach base):
- Best: earth tones (rust, terracotta, olive, warm brown, camel), warm reds (orange-based), mustard, gold, cream, warm green, peach.
- Avoid: stark cool tones (icy blue, true black, bright pink). They clash and read off.
- Gold, brass, copper jewelry. Brown/tan leather.

**Neutral undertone**:
- Most colors work. Best to test on the body — both cool blues and warm browns are typically fine.
- Mid-toned navies, sage, taupe, soft white, dusty pinks, muted reds tend to be especially flattering.

### Contrast level (a separate axis from undertone)

This is RMRS's framework and it's crucial: **how different are your skin, hair, and eye color from each other?**

- **High contrast** — fair skin + dark hair + dark eyes (or dark skin + light eyes / silver hair). Wear high-contrast outfits: white shirt + navy suit, charcoal + crisp light blue, black + white. Strong, dramatic combinations don't overpower because the body already runs at that intensity.
- **Medium contrast** — most men. Brown hair + medium skin + brown/hazel eyes. Tonal combinations work; can also handle moderate contrast.
- **Low contrast** — light hair + light skin + light eyes, OR similar-toned skin/hair (e.g., redhead with fair skin and freckles, bald men with similar-toned skin/eyes). Wear low-contrast outfits — tonal layering, soft neutrals, earthy combinations. Stark contrasts (white + black) overpower the face and pull attention down to the outfit.

### How hair and facial hair color modify the palette

- **Salt-and-pepper / graying hair** raises contrast (cool, often). The palette shifts toward cool grays, blues, charcoals, true whites.
- **Going gray + warm undertone** is the trickiest combination — neither pure-cool nor pure-warm clothing reads right. Steel grays, taupe, muted blues, and sage are usually safer than crisp navy or rust.
- **Dark beard on light skin** raises facial contrast independently of head hair — high-contrast collar/tie combinations work even if the man is otherwise low-contrast.
- **Red beard / auburn beard** — even if hair is darker, the beard pulls warm; warm earth tones (olive, brown, rust) flatter, cool pure colors clash near the face.
- **Bald head** removes hair as a contrast input — the assessment collapses to skin + eye + facial hair.

### Seasonal color analysis

The 12-season system (light spring, true spring, deep autumn, etc.) is **dropped from this framework**. It adds precision in theory but the determination process requires a trained colorist with drape testing in standardized light, which is not replicable through a self-assessment quiz. The undertone × contrast 2-axis system captures ~80% of the styling value with 20% of the determination cost.

## D) Aesthetic Feasibility — Per-Archetype Requirements

This is the load-bearing section for the rewrite. The user's frame — *"most men can't pull off a given aesthetic; some can"* — is correct and is supported by Tanner Guzy's archetype framework, where aesthetic ≠ free choice but is anchored to **body, profession, geography, culture, and personality** in that hierarchy. Guzy: *"the hierarchy of style decisions starts with your body (as aesthetics rules apply everywhere), then moves to archetype, then tribe, and finally incorporates personal taste and location."*

### Tanner Guzy's three archetypes (the meta-framework)

- **Rugged** — physical-world men. Cowboys, lumberjacks, mechanics, blue-collar workers, outdoorsmen. Style is rooted in functional menswear: durability, practicality, weathered materials.
- **Refined** — social/financial-world men. Upper-echelon professionals whose impact is through connections and money. Effortless fine clothing, subtle elegance.
- **Rakish** — rebellious risk-takers. Bold, fashionable, willing to violate convention.

Guzy's key insight: *"the three Style Archetypes are not independent of each other. You'll never find a good style that's 100% Rugged or 0% Rakish. And that's good, because no man is universally one Archetype to the exclusion of the other two. The best styles are the ones that integrate all three in coherent proportions."* For the Cleanmaxxing app this means **archetype is a vector, not a category** — every man has primary/secondary/tertiary mixing, and that mix is what produces a coherent look.

### Aesthetic-specific feasibility (synthesized from Guzy + body-shape sources)

**Rugged** (workwear, Americana, Western, lumberjack-adjacent)
- Body requirements: works best on **broader builds** (athletic to stocky), 5'9"+ helps (the silhouette is bulky and short men look swallowed). Heavyset is fine — rugged is one of the few aesthetics that accommodates volume gracefully.
- Facial requirements: **beard is near-mandatory** for the credible execution. Heavy stubble is the floor; full beard is the ceiling. Patchy or sparse beard growth makes it nearly impossible.
- Hair: medium-length to long; never overly styled.
- Why it fails: slight, clean-shaven men in workwear read costume-y. The aesthetic was developed for and around men who actually do physical labor, and the body and grooming markers signal that history.
- Realistic feasibility: ~40% of men can credibly execute it without modification.

**Classic / Refined** (Old-Money, traditional menswear, business-elegant)
- Body requirements: the **most universally accessible** aesthetic because tailoring fixes most fit issues. Works on slight, athletic, and stocky builds equally well; works on short and tall men with appropriate proportion adjustments. Heavyset is harder but still doable with bespoke or properly altered ready-to-wear.
- Facial requirements: clean-shaven or short well-maintained beard. Long/wild beard fights the aesthetic.
- Hair: cut clean, conservative. Long hair is incompatible.
- Why it fails: poorly-fitted suits, cheap shoes, visible logos, mismatched contrast levels.
- Realistic feasibility: ~75%+ of men can execute it given budget + tailoring.

**Minimalist** (clean lines, neutral palette, slim-but-not-skinny)
- Body requirements: works best on **lean to athletic** builds because the aesthetic is unforgiving — clothes follow the body line closely without disguising. Heavyset and stocky men can do it but need the silhouette adjusted up (looser cuts, structured pieces) which loses some of the minimalist crispness.
- Facial requirements: clean-shaven or very short stubble. Beards conflict with the visual cleanness.
- Hair: short to medium, structured.
- Skin: matters more here because there's nothing visually competing with the face. Acne-prone or uneven complexion shows.
- Why it fails: ill-fit (the aesthetic has no decorative cover for fit problems), too-skinny vs. too-loose miscalibration, and on heavier builds, the unforgiving lines exaggerate rather than flatter.
- Realistic feasibility: ~50% of men can execute well; the rest read either overdressed or hospital-clean.

**Streetwear** (oversized fits, graphic detailing, sneaker culture)
- Body requirements: the **most body-flexible aesthetic** in the source set. Skinny men benefit from oversized tops, muscular men from relaxed fits, larger men from structured-loose silhouettes. Height matters less because the silhouette is itself oversized.
- Facial requirements: minimal — clean-shaven, stubble, or moderate beard all work.
- Cultural/age fit: this is the dimension that fails for the Cleanmaxxing audience (32–45). Streetwear reads age-coded; men over ~38 in heavy streetwear often read as trying too hard, not because of body but because of cohort expectation. Smart-casual or "elevated streetwear" (better fabrics, simpler graphics, fewer logos) ages up.
- Realistic feasibility for the 32–45 cohort: ~30% can wear it credibly; the rest should adopt streetwear-adjacent (sneakers + clean tee + relaxed pants) without the full commitment.

**Preppy** (Ivy League, button-downs, chinos, loafers, cable-knit)
- Body requirements: built for the **lean rectangle to slight-inverted-triangle** body type — the natural-shoulder tailored look without padding. Stocky and heavyset men struggle because the aesthetic was developed around lean-young-collegiate frames.
- Facial requirements: clean-shaven or trimmed beard; wild beards fight the aesthetic.
- Hair: clean, parted, conservative-length. Long hair conflicts.
- Age fit: works through the 32–45 cohort better than streetwear does, especially as "modern preppy" (preppy + minimalism crossover). Pure prep can read costume-y over 40.
- Realistic feasibility: ~50% — strong fit on the right body, weak fit on broader/heavier builds.

**Dark Academia** (tweed, layered knits, oxfords, boots, intellectual signals)
- Body requirements: **moderately accessible**. The layered, structured silhouette works on slight-to-athletic, and is forgiving of soft midsections (layers conceal). Stocky/heavyset works if pieces are properly fitted; the volume of layers can overwhelm shorter men under 5'7".
- Facial requirements: short to medium beard works, clean-shaven works. Hair can be longer here than in classic/minimalist.
- Cultural fit: requires a degree of comfort with the "bookish" cultural signaling — men who read as athletic/blue-collar often look misaligned wearing it. This is a place where the **personality coherence** rule from Guzy bites.
- Realistic feasibility: ~40%.

**Smart Casual** (the dominant mode for the 32–45 cohort)
- Body requirements: **the most body-agnostic of all aesthetics**. Polo or button-down + chinos + clean leather sneakers or loafers works on virtually every body if the fit is right.
- Facial requirements: minimal.
- Realistic feasibility: ~85%+. **This should be the Cleanmaxxing default recommendation** for users without strong archetype indicators.

**Athleisure-adjacent** (technical fabrics, tailored joggers, performance polos, sneakers)
- Body requirements: works on **lean-to-athletic** primarily; the aesthetic visually rewards visible musculature. Soft midsections look worse here than under structured tailoring because the fabrics are clingy. Stocky-muscular works (the aesthetic was partly built for this body).
- Facial: minimal requirements; modern stubble/beard fine.
- Age fit: works through 32–45 if the cuts are tailored rather than gym-rat-baggy.
- Realistic feasibility: ~40% credibly; broader for "athleisure-adjacent at the office" (joggers replaced with chinos, performance polo, sneakers).

### The "earring/long-hair/visible-tattoos" feasibility test

The user's framing is correct and Guzy supports it implicitly via the archetype hierarchy — Rakish elements (visible jewelry, long hair, statement pieces) require either (a) a body/face that anchors them (strong jaw, defined features, height) or (b) other Rakish coherence in the rest of the outfit and life. The standalone Rakish element on an otherwise Refined or Rugged man reads incongruent.

**Long hair** (per the face-shape sources): works strongest on **square** and **oval** faces; round faces benefit because length elongates; long/oblong faces look elongated further (avoid). Combined with face shape, masculine markers (beard, defined jaw) reinforce the look — long hair on a soft jaw and clean shave can read androgynous in a way that pulls outside Cleanmaxxing's audience target.

**Earrings on men**: square/strong-jaw faces benefit from earrings with curved shapes (softens). Round faces should avoid circular earrings (compounds roundness); use teardrop/narrow shapes. Diamond and oval faces are versatile. Heart-shape benefits from wider-bottom earrings. The cultural-context overlay matters more than face shape — at 32–45, single small studs read different from large hoops.

## E) The Accessory Question

### Watches (highest feasibility and impact)
**Wrist size → case diameter rule**:
- ≤6.0" wrist (small) → 34–36mm case.
- 6.0–7.0" (medium, most men) → 38–42mm.
- 7.0–8.0" (large) → 44–46mm.
A watch that overhangs the wrist signals lack of attention. **The most common error is 44mm+ on a 6.5" wrist** — common because brands push large faces and most men buy without measuring. Worth flagging in the assessment: ask wrist circumference.

### Sunglasses (high feasibility, easy to get wrong)
**Rule: opposite shape of face**:
- Square face (strong jaw, equal width/length) → round/oval frames soften.
- Round face → angular frames (rectangle, wayfarer, square-ish aviator) add definition.
- Oval face → most things work.
- Heart face (wide forehead, narrow chin) → wider-at-bottom frames balance — cat-eye, geometric, square.
- Long/oblong → wider, lower-profile frames add horizontal balance (avoid tall narrow frames).

### Earrings
Most men over 32 should default to no earrings unless they're already wearing them as a long-standing identity choice. When worn: small studs minimal disruption; hoops require strong jaw/cheekbone structure to anchor. The aesthetic-coherence rule from Guzy bites — earrings on a Refined-archetype man read incongruent unless the rest of the outfit pulls Rakish.

### Necklaces
Visible necklaces over a tee or open-collar shirt are a Rakish element. They require the rest of the outfit to support it. Thin chain on a fit man works as athleisure-adjacent; large chains read age-coded and often misaligned for the 32–45 cohort.

### Bracelets
Single leather or metal cuff is a low-risk Rakish element. Stacked bracelets require committed Rakish styling elsewhere. Watches paired with bracelets — both on same wrist, lower-key bracelet style.

### Hats
- Round face → fedora, structured trilby (adds angle and height).
- Square face → wide-brim fedora, soft cap with structure.
- Long face → wider brim adds width (Panama, wide-brim sunhat).
- Oval/diamond → most styles work.
- **Baseball caps**: easy fit problem — high-crown caps work better on big-headed and long-faced men; low-profile snapbacks work on smaller heads.
- Cultural fit dominates: fedora reads costume-y on men under 50 in most contexts — flat cap and ball cap are the safer modern defaults.

### Estimate of credible-pull-off proportions for the 32–45 cohort (synthesized inference from sources)
- **Watch**: ~95% can wear one credibly. Highest-feasibility accessory.
- **Sunglasses**: ~90% with face-shape match.
- **Hat (ball cap, flat cap)**: ~80%. Fedora ~15%.
- **Bracelet (single, simple)**: ~50%.
- **Necklace (visible)**: ~25%.
- **Earring**: ~15%.

## F) Garment-Specific Fit Rules

### Pants
- **Rise**: top of waistband one finger-width below the belly button. Mid- to high-rise lengthens legs visually; low-rise shortens them. For shorter men: high-rise mandatory.
- **Taper**: modern taper is roomier in thigh, narrows knee-down. Leg opening between skinny and straight. Streamlines without clinging.
- **Break**: no break (hem grazes shoe top, 1–2" above) for tall/short men chasing height; slight break (rests on shoe) for most men; medium/half break (visible fold over the shoe) for traditional dress; full break (3–4 cm longer, pronounced fold) for tall men or formal old-school looks.
- **Length test**: front of trouser lightly touches the shoe top; back falls near the heel top, slightly longer than the front.
- **Waist test**: when buttoned, you can fit two flat fingers inside the waistband — no more, no less.

### Shirts (dress)
- **Shoulder seam**: aligned with the bony edge of the shoulder where it drops to the arm. Past it = sleeves look long; before it = cuts into the trap.
- **Sleeve length**: ends just past the wrist bone, at the start of the hand. Should show ~½" under jacket sleeve.
- **Collar**: snug but not tight — one finger fits between neck and buttoned collar. No visible gap when buttoned (kills the look).
- **Chest**: smooth coverage, no buttoning gap, no horizontal pull lines.
- **Hem**: long enough to stay tucked through normal movement; short enough untucked to end at the belt line, never past hips.

### T-shirts
- **Shoulder seam**: same rule as dress shirts — at the shoulder bone edge.
- **Sleeve length**: roughly mid-bicep for muscular men (shows the arm); short of mid-bicep for slight men (not gathered fabric at the elbow).
- **Hem**: ends at mid-fly to belt line, never below hip. The "long tee" trend has aged out for the 32–45 cohort.
- **Body fit**: skim, don't cling. A slight drape across the chest and a half-thumb of pull-away at the waist is the modern fit.

### Jackets / Blazers
- **Shoulder fit**: the single most-cited rule across every source. The shoulder seam ends exactly at the bony edge of the natural shoulder, not before, not past. Gentleman's Gazette: *"making shoulders bigger or smaller is not recommended as alterations, since the structure of a jacket shoulder is complex enough that reshaping it involves major surgery."* **If the shoulder doesn't fit, the jacket doesn't fit. Don't buy it.**
- **Sleeve length**: jacket sleeve ends at the wrist break; ¼"–½" of shirt cuff shows underneath. The shirt-cuff visibility is functional (protects the jacket from skin oils) and signal of attention.
- **Length**: covers the seat. Hem hits between the second knuckle and the first knuckle of a relaxed-arm fist. Modern cuts run shorter; classic cuts cover the seat fully.
- **Lapel width**: should match the man's body — wider lapels for broader chests, narrower for slimmer. Should also match tie width and pant width within the same outfit.
- **Buttoning**: when buttoned, the X-pull between buttons indicates too tight. Pooling fabric below the buttoning point indicates too loose. Single-breasted: top button only when standing, never the lower button(s).

### Sweaters
- **Shoulder seam**: on the acromion bone, same as shirts. Higher = sleeve cuts armpit. Lower = drops to upper arm and pools.
- **Sleeve cuff**: at the wrist; with shirt underneath, ½" before the thumb base.
- **Hem**: at the mid-fly or just below the hip bone — long enough to stay tucked, short enough to not bunch under jeans.
- **Body fit**: skim through the chest, no horizontal wrinkles across the midsection, no button-pull on cardigans.

## G) ROI Tiering of Garments

The user's framing — spend on shoes/watch/sunglasses, fit-over-price on pants/shirts/sweaters — **strongly aligns with the source consensus**. Specifically:

### The investment hierarchy (ranked by ROI)

**Tier 1 — Splurge (10-year+ investments)**
1. **Shoes**. RMRS: *"You cannot look wealthy in cheap shoes, and if you're going to spend money on one thing (apart from tailoring), shoes should be it. A quality pair of leather shoes with Goodyear Welt or Blake Stitching will last 10+ years."* Simon Crompton (Permanent Style) makes the same case as a multi-decade investment proposition. The reason: shoes are visible, daily-worn, and quality is impossible to fake.
2. **Outerwear** (overcoat, leather jacket, technical shell). Worn over many outfits, daily visible in cold weather, hardest to fake quality. Westwood Hart: outerwear delivers some of the highest ROI per garment because of repeat-wear.
3. **Watch**. RMRS: *"Every man should invest in a quality watch... it's a reflection of your personality, with all successful men having quality watches in common."*

**Tier 2 — Quality matters but tailor saves you**
4. **Suits / blazers**. A $400 suit with $200 of tailoring beats a $1,200 off-the-rack suit. Tailoring is the multiplier. Every source agrees: budget for tailoring at 15–20% of purchase price minimum.
5. **Sunglasses**. Daily-worn, frame-the-face, easy to get wrong. Mid-tier (Persol/Ray-Ban-and-up) is enough; ultra-luxury isn't ROI-positive.

**Tier 3 — Fit-over-price**
6. **Trousers / chinos / jeans**. Fit, fabric, and a basic level of construction matter much more than brand or price. A $90 well-tailored pair of chinos beats a $300 pair off-the-rack. Replace every 2–4 years.
7. **Shirts / polos**. Same logic. Fit at the shoulder, sleeve, and chest is everything. Gentleman's Gazette: *"Get your clothes tailored — tailoring makes low to medium-end clothing look bespoke and high-end."*
8. **Sweaters / knitwear**. Fabric quality matters more than brand at this tier — wool/cashmere blends from mid-tier brands beat synthetic-blend luxury brands.

**Tier 4 — Replaceable**
9. **T-shirts / undershirts**. High-rotation, replaced often. Mid-tier (Uniqlo, Asket, Buck Mason) is the sweet spot.
10. **Underwear / socks**. Replaced annually.

### Why this ordering is correct
The source consensus is that ROI is a function of **(a) visibility × (b) durability × (c) gap-between-cheap-and-quality**. Shoes win on all three: visible in every outfit, last 10+ years if quality, and the gap between $80 shoes and $400 shoes is dramatic and visible. T-shirts lose on all three: rarely the focal point, replaced every 1–2 years anyway, and the gap between $20 and $80 tees is small.

**The rule the user articulated is correct and well-supported.**

## I) Where the Experts Disagree With Mainstream Style Advice

Counterintuitive takes that emerged repeatedly across the source set and that are correct enough to adopt:

### 1. "Buy quality basics" is overrated; **fit beats fabric** for the bottom 70% of the wardrobe.
Mainstream advice: invest in everything. Expert reality (Gentleman's Gazette, RMRS, Westwood Hart): a $90 well-tailored pair of chinos beats a $300 untailored pair every time. **Tailoring is a higher-ROI spend than upgrading fabric quality on most garments.**

### 2. "Skinny fit" is wrong for most men, including muscular men.
Mainstream advice: muscular men should wear slim cuts to "show off" the body. Expert reality: skinny jeans on a muscular man make the upper body look disproportionately wide, get stuck on calves, and pull a body that is already inverted-triangle into a more extreme inverted-triangle. The right cut for muscular men is **slim-but-tapered**, not skinny.

### 3. "Black goes with everything" is wrong if you have low contrast.
Mainstream advice: black is universally flattering. Expert reality (RMRS contrast theory): high-contrast outfits on a low-contrast man (light skin, light hair, light eyes) overpower the face and pull attention to the outfit. Stark white-and-black on a redhead with freckles is visually wrong. Tonal layering wins for low-contrast men.

### 4. "Vest bottom button undone" is fashion myth, not rule.
Gentleman's Gazette explicitly debunks: the rule comes from King Edward VII being too fat to button his vests. If your vest fits properly and looks better fully buttoned, button it. Inherited rules without function are noise.

### 5. "More expensive watches are always better" is wrong; wrist size is the gating factor.
Mainstream advice: spend up on watches. Expert reality: a $200 watch correctly sized for a 6.5" wrist looks better than a $5,000 watch that's 44mm overhanging the same wrist. Get the size right first.

### 6. "Suits should be slim" is era-locked.
Gentleman's Gazette repeatedly notes that "slim everything" is a 2010s artifact and is already aging out. The source's preferred default is moderate cut — neither slim nor relaxed — because *"a non-extreme look will always be more timeless."*

### 7. "Match your belt to your shoes" is too rigid.
Belt-shoe matching is a low-contrast technique. For men who need a horizontal break (short torso / long legs), contrasting belt and shoes is a styling tool, not a violation. The mainstream rule conflates "default" with "always."

### 8. Most "color rules" should be replaced by undertone × contrast.
Mainstream advice produces lists like "men shouldn't wear pink" and "navy is the universal suit." Expert reality: pink looks great on cool-undertone men, terrible on some warm-undertone men. The undertone × contrast model collapses dozens of arbitrary color rules into two diagnostic axes that produce per-individual answers.

### 9. "Dress for the body you have, not the body you want" — a quiet expert consensus.
This is everywhere implicitly and it's the foundation of the body-reality-driven approach Cleanmaxxing is shifting toward. Most style content sells aspirational aesthetic; the experts sell silhouette correction. Real pull-off requires alignment, not transformation. This is the through-line of the entire source set.

### 10. "Tailor first, shop later" is more important than any specific garment rule.
RMRS, Gentleman's Gazette, Westwood Hart, and Permanent Style all converge on the same conclusion: a man's most important clothing relationship is with his tailor, not with any brand. Off-the-rack is rarely correct. Tailoring is the multiplier on every other variable in this framework.
