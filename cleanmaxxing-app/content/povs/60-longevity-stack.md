__Longevity Stack: Rapamycin, Metformin, NAD+ Precursors, Resveratrol__

*The biohacker and anti-aging compound class — what the human evidence actually shows*

__Why This Document__

The peptide documents (04 and 40) cover compounds used primarily for body composition, recovery, and skin — the muscle and grooming side of looksmaxxing. This document covers a different cluster: the anti-aging and longevity compounds that show up in biohacker discourse but were never specifically about looksmaxxing. Rapamycin, metformin, NAD+ precursors (NMN and NR), and resveratrol get asked about in the same conversation because the same audience finds them at the same time, but the goals are different and the evidence picture is materially different from the peptide stack.

The short version is that the animal data on most of these compounds is more interesting than the human data, and the translation to clinically meaningful human outcomes has been smaller and slower than the marketing implies. None of this is a looksmax intervention. It is longevity-adjacent self-experimentation with thinner evidence than most users assume, and in one case — metformin — there is an active interaction concern with the training a Cleanmaxxing user is already doing.

__Rapamycin — Low-Dose Off-Label for Longevity__

__What it is__

Rapamycin (sirolimus) is an mTOR inhibitor originally approved as an immunosuppressant for organ transplant patients. The longevity interest comes from a different dosing protocol entirely — weekly low doses, typically 5–8 mg per week in supervised longevity protocols, rather than the daily transplant doses. The conceptual basis is that intermittent mTOR inhibition mimics the cellular signaling of caloric restriction without the daily immunosuppression that comes with continuous high-dose use.

__The mouse data__

Rapamycin is one of the most consistently lifespan-extending compounds in the NIA Interventions Testing Program, which independently replicates candidate interventions across multiple mouse strains and starting ages. Lifespan extension has been demonstrated in male and female mice, in young and old mice, across multiple labs. This is unusual — most candidate longevity compounds fail ITP replication. The preclinical case for rapamycin is the strongest in this entire document.

__The human data__

Thin. The PEARL trial (Participatory Evaluation of Aging with Rapamycin for Longevity), published in *Aging* (Moel et al. 2025, PMID 40188830) and led by Matt Kaeberlein's group, was the first randomized controlled trial of low-dose rapamycin in healthy adults. It found modest improvements in some physical function and well-being measures with acceptable tolerability over roughly a year of dosing. It was not a lifespan trial — those would take decades — and the sample size was small. Earlier work by Joan Mannick on rapalogs (everolimus and RTB101) showed improved influenza vaccine response in older adults, supporting the idea that intermittent mTOR inhibition can enhance rather than suppress immune function. The phase 2b/phase 3 follow-up trials (Mannick et al. 2021, *The Lancet Healthy Longevity*, PMID 33977284) extended that evidence base to broader immune-function endpoints in older adults. That finding is counterintuitive given the daily-dose immunosuppression context and is the basis for a lot of the current longevity-medicine enthusiasm.

__What is not known__

Whether low-dose rapamycin extends human lifespan or healthspan. Whether the dose ranges used by current longevity practitioners are the right ones. Long-term safety in healthy adults across decades. Whether the small-trial benefits that exist will hold up at scale.

__Side effects__

Mouth ulcers (canker sores) are the most common, often dose-related and reversible. Lipid changes with LDL and triglyceride elevations are commonly reported. Potential insulin resistance with chronic use. Theoretical immunosuppression risk that may or may not manifest at intermittent dosing.

__Regulatory and access__

Prescription medication. Off-label use for longevity exists primarily through longevity-focused clinics. Not available through legitimate supplement channels. Compounded versions exist but raise the same sourcing-uncertainty issue as research peptides.

__The honest read__

Rapamycin has the strongest preclinical case in this category, the most prominent advocates in longevity medicine, and the thinnest human outcome data. It is a reasonable thing for an informed adult to discuss with a longevity-focused physician after the fundamentals — sleep, training, nutrition, social relationships, sun exposure, stress management — are already in place. None of those fundamentals are pharmaceuticals, and all of them have larger documented effects on healthspan than any compound in this category currently does.

__Metformin — The Fitness-Audience Caveat Most Discussions Skip__

__What it is__

Metformin is a first-line type 2 diabetes medication that reduces hepatic glucose output and improves insulin sensitivity. The longevity interest stems from older observational data suggesting diabetics on metformin had lower all-cause mortality than expected, with some studies finding mortality similar to or lower than non-diabetic controls. This drove the proposed TAME trial (Targeting Aging with Metformin), led by Nir Barzilai, designed to test whether metformin in non-diabetic older adults reduces the incidence of multiple age-related diseases. TAME has been in funding and design phases for years; it is not the kind of trial that produces fast answers.

__The mouse data__

Less consistent than rapamycin. Metformin has not reliably extended lifespan in healthy mice in ITP replication efforts. Some studies show effects in combination with other interventions; standalone lifespan effects are weaker than rapamycin's.

__The fitness-audience problem__

This is the part most longevity discussions skip and the part this audience needs to know. Konopka and colleagues (2019, *Aging Cell*, PMID 30548390) demonstrated that metformin blunted the mitochondrial and cardiorespiratory adaptations from aerobic training in older adults. A subset of follow-up work has shown blunted resistance training response on metformin in some contexts. The mechanism is plausible — metformin and exercise both activate AMPK, but metformin appears to interfere with the mitochondrial biogenesis signal that exercise normally produces, possibly by suppressing the same hormetic stress that training depends on.

For a population whose primary intervention is training, this is not a small concern. A user adding metformin for longevity reasons that are themselves not well-established may be reducing the training adaptations that have the largest documented effect on long-term health.

__Side effects__

GI distress is common and often the limiting factor. Long-term use is associated with vitamin B12 deficiency. Lactic acidosis is rare but real, especially in patients with declining kidney function.

__Regulatory and access__

Prescription. Generic and inexpensive when prescribed.

__The honest read__

The longevity case for metformin in non-diabetic adults is weaker than the rapamycin case. The fitness-audience case is actively complicated: there is real signal that metformin may blunt the adaptive response to the training a Cleanmaxxing user is already doing. A user pursuing physical improvement through training has a reason to think twice before adding metformin for longevity reasons that have not been established to begin with.

__NMN and NR — NAD+ Precursors__

__What they are__

Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR) are precursors that the body converts to NAD+, a coenzyme involved in mitochondrial function, sirtuin activity, and DNA repair. NAD+ levels decline with age. The hypothesis is that restoring NAD+ via supplementation will reverse some age-related decline.

__The mouse data__

Restoring NAD+ through NMN or NR improves multiple markers in aged mice — mitochondrial function, exercise capacity, insulin sensitivity. The work by Shin-ichiro Imai and David Sinclair drove much of the field's interest and the bulk of the public discourse.

__The human data__

NMN and NR do reliably raise blood NAD+ levels in humans — that part replicates. The downstream clinical effects have been the disappointment. Trials measuring exercise performance, insulin sensitivity, cognitive function, and other outcomes have generally found either no significant effect or modest effects that do not match the preclinical promise. The translation from "raises NAD+" to "produces meaningful health outcome" has been weaker than expected across multiple endpoints.

__Regulatory status__

NR is sold as a supplement (ChromaDex's Tru Niagen is the most visible brand). NMN's status in the US is unsettled — the FDA in 2022 took the position that NMN could not be marketed as a dietary supplement because it had been investigated as a drug, though enforcement has been inconsistent and product remains widely available.

__Side effects__

Generally well-tolerated at typical supplement doses. Methylation-related concerns are theoretical and unclear.

__The honest read__

NAD+ precursors raise NAD+. Whether that produces outcomes the user cares about — energy, performance, healthspan — has not been demonstrated in the human trials that exist so far. The supplement industry is years ahead of the data. The most defensible framing is that this is plausible biology with disappointing human translation to date.

__Resveratrol — The Trial-and-Disappointment Cycle__

__What it is__

A polyphenol found in red wine, grapes, and Japanese knotweed, promoted as a sirtuin activator after early Sinclair-lab work showed lifespan effects in yeast and dose-dependent benefits in obese mice on high-fat diets. The "red wine longevity" narrative is the consumer-facing version of this work.

__The human data__

Broadly disappointing. Bioavailability is poor — most oral resveratrol is rapidly metabolized. Trials in healthy and metabolically compromised adults have found small or null effects on insulin sensitivity, cardiovascular markers, and inflammation. A few small trials show signal in specific contexts (postmenopausal bone density, vascular function in older adults), but the dramatic claims from the early preclinical work have not translated to the general healthy-adult population at achievable oral doses.

__Side effects__

Generally safe at supplement doses. Some interaction with anticoagulants. Higher doses can cause GI distress.

__The honest read__

Resveratrol is the canonical example of the longevity-supplement trial-and-disappointment cycle. Strong preclinical signal, marketing built around it, human trials show small or null effects, the supplement keeps selling because the marketing established faster than the data corrected. There is no compelling reason for someone focused on physical improvement to spend money here.

__The Cleanmaxxing Position on the Longevity Stack__

None of these compounds is a looksmax intervention. They will not change how a user looks in a meaningful way over any timeframe a user can perceive. They are longevity- and healthspan-adjacent self-experiments with thinner human evidence than the marketing implies and, in metformin's case, an active interaction concern with the training a Cleanmaxxing user is already doing.

The framework from the peptide documents applies here too. The men with the most polished longevity protocols are without exception running a strong foundation underneath them. Sleep, training, nutrition, social relationships, sun exposure, and stress management have larger documented effects on healthspan than any compound in this document. Compounds amplify a foundation. They do not substitute for one.

__Who Might Have a Reasonable Case for Engaging With This Category__

- Adults who already have the fundamentals dialed in and are working with a longevity-focused physician who can run baseline labs, monitor for known side effects, and provide informed dose guidance
- Adults with specific clinical pictures where one of these compounds has a more defensible case than its general-longevity marketing — a clinician would identify these, not a self-selected protocol

__Who Should Not Touch This Category__

- Anyone whose training, nutrition, or sleep is not already consistent — the foundation will outproduce any of these compounds by a wide margin
- Anyone using metformin while pursuing serious physical training goals without understanding the adaptive-blunting evidence
- Anyone sourcing rapamycin outside of supervised channels
- Anyone whose interest in this category is driven primarily by content from people selling the supplements

__Tier Placement__

__The longevity stack is not a looksmax category. The compound with the best preclinical case (rapamycin) has the thinnest human outcome data. The compounds with the most marketing (NMN, resveratrol) have produced disappointing or null human trials. Metformin has an active conflict with the training response that this audience depends on. The honest framing is that this is informational territory for users curious about the space, not a recommendation, not a protocol, and not something that competes with the foundation work that drives both looksmaxxing and healthspan outcomes.__
